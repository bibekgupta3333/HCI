Do People Trust AI More When It Explains Itself?
A Literature Review on Explanation Depth, Trust, and Automation Bias in AI Decision
Interfaces
Deepthi Reddy Chelladi, Siwani Sah, Bibek Gupta, Afzal Hossain Sohag, Md Shamimul Islam, and
Mohamed Sylla

1. Introduction and Problem Statement
Artificial intelligence now supports decisions in high-stakes areas such as lending, hiring, health
care, and criminal justice. In consumer lending, automated models screen applications and
recommend whether a loan should be approved or rejected, but a human reviewer is often still
responsible for accepting or overriding that recommendation. What the interface shows about the
AI's reasoning can influence whether people agree with the system, how much they trust it,
whether they think the outcome is fair, and, most importantly, whether they notice when the
system is wrong. The stakes are also regulatory. In 2022, the Consumer Financial Protection
Bureau stated that lenders must give applicants the specific reasons for a denial even when the
decision comes from a complex “black-box” algorithm [7]. The Bureau withdrew that circular in
May 2025, but the question it raised, how a lender can explain an algorithmic decision to the
person affected, remains central to AI in lending.
A common response to these concerns is explainable AI, the idea that AI systems should
explain their outputs so people can understand and evaluate them [9]. Much of this work carries
an unstated assumption that more explanation leads to better, more appropriate trust. Research in
human factors and HCI suggests that this assumption is too simple. People already have a
well-documented tendency to over-rely on automated aids [24, 26], and recent experiments
found that explanations increased acceptance of AI recommendations whether those
recommendations were right or wrong [1]. An explanation may make a wrong decision sound
more convincing instead of making the error easier to catch.
The problem our project addresses is that it remains unclear how the depth of an explanation
affects user behavior and perception when the AI is sometimes incorrect. By depth we mean a
range from no explanation (Condition A, a black box), to a fixed written reason (Condition B, a
static explanation), to an interactive breakdown the user can explore factor by factor (Condition
C). Most prior studies compare the presence of an explanation to its absence, or compare
different explanation algorithms, and fewer compare formats that differ in both depth and
interactivity within the same task while also including deliberately incorrect AI decisions. This
review supports a within-subjects study of loan decisions using these three conditions. Sections 3
to 7 review trust in automation, human-centered XAI, the effects of explanations on reliance,
explanation depth and interactivity, and fairness perceptions. Section 8 discusses methodological
implications, and Section 9 states the research gap and our hypotheses.
2. Scope and Criteria for Inclusion
We searched the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, and the journal Human
Factors using combinations of “trust in automation,” “automation bias,” “overreliance,”
2
“explainable AI user study,” “interactive explanation,” and “algorithmic fairness perception,”
and followed the reference lists of the most relevant papers. A source was included if it (a) is a
foundational or highly cited work that defines a concept central to our study, such as trust,
automation bias, or explanation; (b) is a peer-reviewed empirical study measuring how
explanations affect trust, reliance, decision accuracy, or fairness judgments; or (c) provides a
validated instrument or method we plan to use. We gave priority to CHI, IUI, CSCW, FAccT, and
Human Factors, where most empirical work on human-AI decision making appears, and
excluded papers that proposed interpretability algorithms without any human evaluation, except
SHAP, which directly shapes how feature-based explanations are shown to users. The final set
contains 29 sources published between 1978 and 2023. Older sources were kept when they
introduced ideas, such as automation bias or placebic explanation, that newer AI studies still
build on.
3. Trust in Automation and Automation Bias
3.1 Trust and Appropriate Reliance
Lee and See [19] provide the definition of trust most often used in this area: an attitude that an
automated agent will help a person reach their goals in a situation of uncertainty and
vulnerability. Their most important contribution for our project is calibration. The goal of good
design is not maximum trust but trust that matches what the system can actually do. When trust
exceeds the system's capability, people misuse the automation, and when it falls short, they
disuse it. This extends the taxonomy of Parasuraman and Riley [24], who separated appropriate
use of automation from misuse, disuse, and abuse. Together, these papers explain why our study
measures agreement on correct and incorrect AI decisions separately, since high agreement is
only good when it is concentrated on the correct cases.
Hoff and Bashir [13] reviewed 127 empirical studies and organized the factors that shape
trust into three layers: dispositional trust (a general tendency to trust automation), situational
trust (the context and task), and learned trust (what a person learns from interacting with a
particular system). Design features such as transparency fall under learned trust, which is exactly
where explanation depth sits. The model also warns that individual differences add noise, one
reason a within-subjects design is attractive. Learned trust changes quickly. Dzindolet et al. [10]
found that people start with a positive bias toward automated aids, that seeing the aid err lowers
trust sharply, and that telling people why the aid might err increased trust and reliance even
though its accuracy had not changed. This is one of the earliest signs that explanations can raise
reliance independently of accuracy.
3.2 Automation Bias
Automation bias is the tendency to use automated recommendations as a shortcut for careful
information seeking and processing. Skitka et al. [26] separated omission errors, in which people
fail to act because the automation did not alert them, from commission errors, in which people
follow an automated directive even though other available information contradicts it.
Commission errors are exactly what our planted wrong cases are designed to detect, since each
wrong AI decision contradicts the applicant's own numbers on the screen. Parasuraman and
Manzey [23] concluded that automation bias appears in both novices and experts and cannot be
prevented by training or instructions alone. Green and Chen [12] found related problems in an
algorithm-in-the-loop experiment that included a lending scenario: participants could not
effectively evaluate the accuracy of the algorithm's predictions and did not calibrate their
3
reliance to its actual performance. A limitation is that most classic studies involve trained
operators and automation that offers no explanation, whereas modern AI interfaces also argue for
their answers.
3.3 Measuring Trust
Our self-report measure is the empirically developed 12-item Trust in Automation scale [15].
Hoffman et al. [14] argued that trust in XAI should be measured through both self-report and
behavior, because what people say and what they do often differ. We therefore pair the scale with
behavioral agreement, separated into correct and incorrect cases, which gives a direct measure of
the appropriate reliance that Lee and See [19] identify as the construct that matters.
4. Explainable AI From a Human-Centered Perspective
Early XAI research focused on algorithms. Feature-attribution methods such as SHAP assign
each input a contribution to a prediction [21], and the factor-by-factor breakdown in our
interactive condition, which shows income against a threshold, credit history length, and
debt-to-income ratio, is essentially a user-facing version of this idea. In the terms of Doshi-Velez
and Kim [9], our project is a human-grounded evaluation, testing how real people respond to
explanations on a simplified but realistic task.
Miller [22] argued that XAI researchers largely ignored decades of work in philosophy,
psychology, and cognitive science on how people explain things to each other. Human
explanations are contrastive (why this outcome instead of another), selective (a few key causes
rather than a complete list), and social (a conversation adjusted to what the listener knows).
These properties map onto our conditions. The static explanation in Condition B is selective,
giving two short reasons, while the interactive explanation in Condition C is more contrastive,
placing each factor next to the threshold it passed or failed, and more conversational, since the
user decides what to ask about. Lim et al. [20] similarly found that “why” and “why not”
explanations produced better understanding and stronger trust than other explanation types,
which supports the “Why?” control in Condition C.
5. Effects of Explanations on Trust, Agreement, and Error Detection
5.1 Evidence That Explanations Help
Some studies found clear benefits. Kulesza et al. [17] showed that explanations helped users
build more accurate mental models of a recommender system. Cheng et al. [5] tested several
explanation interfaces with 199 non-expert participants using a university admissions algorithm
and found that interactive and “white-box” explanations both improved comprehension, with the
interactive approach improving it most but taking more time. Surprisingly, trust was not affected
by the interface or by how well participants understood the algorithm, a strong hint that
understanding, trust, and reliance must be measured separately.
5.2 Evidence That Explanations Do Not Improve, or Even Harm, Decisions
A growing number of controlled experiments suggest that explanations often fail to improve
decision quality. Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. [25] ran pre-registered experiments with about 3,800
participants who predicted apartment prices with a model's help. A more transparent model
helped people simulate its predictions but made them worse at detecting when it had made a
large mistake, which the authors attributed to information overload. Zhang et al. [29], using an
income prediction task close to our lending domain, found that showing a confidence score
4
helped people calibrate their trust, while local feature-based explanations did not. The most
direct evidence for the backfire effect our project tests comes from Bansal et al. [1], who found
across several tasks that explanations increased acceptance of the AI's recommendations
regardless of whether they were correct, and added nothing beyond simply displaying the AI's
confidence. Bussone et al. [4] reported a similar pattern in a clinical decision support prototype.
Clinicians who saw comprehensive explanations trusted the system more but also agreed with
more of its incorrect diagnoses. Like our study, it used a Wizard-of-Oz prototype with planted
errors, although with only seven clinicians its findings need testing with larger samples.
There is also a psychological explanation for this effect. Langer et al. [18] found that people
complied with a small request almost as often when it came with a meaningless reason (“because
I have to make copies”) as when it came with a real one. Eiband et al. [11] found that placebic
explanations containing no real information produced trust similar to real explanations in a
nutrition recommender, though their study had only 30 participants. This is highly relevant to
Condition B. A fixed sentence such as “Rejected because income is below threshold” sounds
justified whether or not it is true, which may explain why static explanations can increase
agreement with wrong decisions.
5.3 When Explanations Can Reduce Overreliance
Not all recent evidence is negative. Buçinca et al. [3] tested cognitive forcing functions, such as
asking people to decide before seeing the AI's answer or to request its recommendation on
demand. These reduced overreliance compared with simple XAI designs, although participants
rated them less favorably. Vasconcelos et al. [28] offered a cost-benefit account of the mixed
findings. Across five studies with 731 participants, explanations reduced overreliance when they
made checking the AI's answer much easier than solving the task alone, suggesting that earlier
null results may reflect explanations that did not lower the cost of verification enough. These
studies matter for Condition C. The “Why?” button requires an action, which may act as a mild
forcing function, and the breakdown places the applicant's numbers next to the thresholds, which
makes verification cheap.
6. Explanation Depth and Interactivity
How much explanation is enough is itself contested. Kulesza et al. [17] varied the soundness and
completeness of explanations and found that completeness mattered more for building accurate
mental models, while heavily oversimplified explanations increased mental demand and reduced
trust in the explanations themselves. Too much detail, however, can overload users [25]. Springer
and Whittaker [27] addressed this tension in four studies of an emotion analytics system.
Transparency helped users build a model of how the algorithm worked but could lower their
accuracy judgments when it exposed the system's errors, and the authors recommended
progressive disclosure, starting with simplified feedback and revealing more detail on request.
Condition C follows this approach by showing the decision first and the breakdown only when
the user clicks “Why?” Interactivity, however, does not guarantee careful thinking. Kaur et al.
[16] found that data scientists using interpretability tools often over-trusted them and took the
visual output at face value. If experienced data scientists can over-trust detailed, interactive
explanations, lay users in a loan scenario may do the same. The literature therefore makes two
competing predictions: interactive explanations may support verification by lowering its cost, or
they may create a stronger illusion of understanding because they look more thorough.

7. Perceptions of Fairness in Algorithmic Decisions
Fairness is especially relevant in lending, where decisions directly affect people's lives. Colquitt
[6] validated a four-dimensional model of organizational justice: distributive, procedural,
interpersonal, and informational. Informational justice concerns whether people receive adequate
and truthful explanations, which suggests that explanation depth should affect perceived fairness.
Binns et al. [2] tested this using scenarios that included loan decisions and found that explanation
style mattered mainly when participants were exposed to several styles. When they saw only one,
differences between scenarios hid any effect of explanation. Dodge et al. [8], with more than 160
participants and four explanation types for a recidivism model, found that some styles were seen
as inherently less fair and that individual differences shaped reactions to each style. Richer
explanations should therefore increase perceived fairness, but such judgments are sensitive to
study design.
8. Methodological Implications
The reviewed work also shapes our design. We use a Wizard-of-Oz approach in which AI
decisions are pre-scripted, as Bussone et al. [4] did. This gives full control over which decisions
are wrong and ensures that every wrong decision contradicts the applicant's own numbers, which
is necessary for measuring error detection. The trade-off is ecological validity, since real AI
errors are rarely this clear-cut, so our results should be read as a best case for error detection. A
within-subjects design offers more statistical power with fewer participants, but Binns et al. [2]
show that exposure to several explanation styles can itself change judgments, and Dzindolet et al.
[10] show that observing an AI error lowers trust for the rest of a session. We therefore
counterbalance condition order, case order, and the position of the wrong cases. Following
Hoffman et al. [14], we measure trust through self-report, behavior, and time-to-decision.
Time-to-decision is useful because fast decisions may reflect heuristic acceptance of the AI's
answer, while the cognitive forcing work of Buçinca et al. [3] suggests that slower, more
effortful decisions may reflect actual verification. Table 1 summarizes the key empirical studies.

8.1 Limitations of the Reviewed Literature
Three limitations cut across the studies reviewed here. First, many of the strongest findings come
from short online tasks with crowd workers, such as apartment pricing [25] or sentiment analysis
[1], where participants have little at stake, so transfer to lending is uncertain. Second, several of
the studies most directly relevant to our backfire hypothesis are small: Bussone et al. [4]
observed only seven clinicians and Eiband et al. [11] only 30 participants, so their conclusions
should be treated as suggestive rather than settled. Third, studies measure “trust” in different
ways, from questionnaires [15] to agreement rates [1] to comprehension tests [5], which makes it
hard to compare effect sizes across papers. Our study addresses this by measuring all of these in
the same participants on one task.
9. Research Gap and Hypotheses
The goal is calibrated rather than maximum trust [19], and people are prone to commission errors
when automation is wrong [26]. Explanations frequently increase agreement with AI regardless
of correctness [1, 4], possibly because they work partly as persuasive cues [11, 18]. Yet
explanations that lower the cost of verification or require active engagement can reduce
overreliance [3, 28], while very detailed explanations can overload users [25]. We identify four
gaps. First, few studies compare three levels of depth, including an interactive format, within one
design. Second, few combine explanation depth with deliberately incorrect AI decisions that
users can verify on screen. Third, trust, agreement, error detection, and fairness are usually
studied separately [2]. Fourth, predictions about interactive explanations compete [16, 28]. H1
and H2 concern responses across all cases, while H3a and H3b concern incorrect AI decisions
only.
H1. Overall agreement with the AI will increase with explanation depth, from A to B
to C.
H2. Self-reported trust [15] will be higher in B and C than in A.
H3a. On incorrect AI decisions, participants will agree more often in B than in A,
reflecting a persuasive effect of fixed explanations.
H3b. On incorrect AI decisions, participants will agree less often in C than in B,
because the breakdown makes verification easier. Because overload findings
predict the opposite, we treat H3b as exploratory.
H4. Perceived fairness will be highest in C and lowest in A.
H5. Time-to-decision will be longest in C, and longer decision times will be
associated with better detection of incorrect AI decisions.
10. Conclusion
People likely do trust AI more when it explains itself, but the real issue is whether explanations
help them trust it appropriately, more when it is right and less when it is wrong. Static
explanations may act as persuasive cues, while interactive ones may help people catch errors or
may overload them. By comparing black-box, static, and interactive explanations within
7
subjects, planting verifiable errors in a loan-approval task, and measuring agreement, trust,
fairness, and decision time together, our study will show whether more explanation leads to
better human-AI decisions or simply to more agreement.
